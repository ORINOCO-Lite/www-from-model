  description: The brain regions supporting sustained attention (sustained attention
    network; SAN) and mind-wandering (default-mode network; DMN) have been extensively
    studied. Nevertheless, this knowledge has not yet been translated into advanced
    brain-based attention training protocols. Here, we used network-based real-time
    functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to provide healthy individuals with
    information about current activity levels in SAN and DMN. Specifically, 15 participants
    trained to control the difference between SAN and DMN hemodynamic activity and completed
    behavioral attention tests before and after neurofeedback training. Through training,
    participants improved controlling the differential SAN-DMN feedback signal, which
    was accomplished mainly through deactivating DMN. After training, participants were
    able to apply learned self-regulation of the differential feedback signal even when
    feedback was no longer available (i.e., during transfer runs). The neurofeedback
    group improved in sustained attention after training, although this improvement
    was temporally limited and rarely exceeded mere practice effects that were controlled
    by a test-retest behavioral control group. The learned self-regulation and the behavioral
    outcomes suggest that neurofeedback training of differential SAN and DMN activity
    has the potential to become a non-invasive and non-pharmacological tool to enhance
    attention and mitigate specific attention deficits.
